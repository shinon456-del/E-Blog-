My Learning Journey in Formulating Research Problems

the winding path from a vague idea to a defensible study

During Weeks 3 and 4 of Technology Research 1, I learned that creating a research problem is not just about choosing a topic that sounds interesting. At first, I thought making research titles and questions would be simple, but I realized that every part of the research has to be connected. The research problem, objectives, research questions, variables, and scope should all support each other.

Recognizing a Real Research Problem

One of the things I learned was how to identify a real research problem. A research problem should show a gap between the ideal situation and the current reality, and there should be a possible intervention or approach that can be studied. This helped me understand that a simple problem or complaint is not automatically a research problem. It needs to be something that can be investigated through data and proper research methods.

Aligning Research Questions and Objectives

I also learned the importance of making Research Questions and Research Objectives aligned. The module explained that research questions should be clear, measurable, and connected to the objectives. This became important when our group worked on our proposed research problems. For example, in our first research title, "The Effect of Digital Learning Activities on the Digital Literacy Skills of First-Year BTLED-ICT Students," we identified digital learning activities as the independent variable and digital literacy skills as the dependent variable. Our study uses a quantitative quasi-experimental design and compares students' skills before and after the activities.

Where it got hard

One challenge I experienced was revising our research questions. At first, some questions were not properly focused or did not clearly show what we wanted to measure. We had to revise them so that they would match our objectives and quantitative research design. In particular, we revised questions about the digital literacy skills of first-year BTLED-ICT students before the intervention and their improvement after the intervention. The final questions use measurable data such as pre-test scores, post-test scores, and mean gain scores.

Delimitations vs. Limitations

Another important lesson for me was understanding delimitations. Before this topic, I sometimes confused delimitations with limitations. I learned that delimitations are boundaries intentionally set by the researcher, such as the target participants, research setting, scope of the study, and time period. Limitations, on the other hand, are problems or conditions that are outside the researcher's control.

Evidence Over Assumption

I also learned that research should be based on evidence instead of personal assumptions. Scientific thinking requires researchers to collect measurable data, question assumptions, and remain objective when analyzing results. This changed how I look at research because I realized that we should not simply say that an activity or technology is effective just because we think it is. We need actual evidence to support the claim.

Where This Leaves Me

Overall, these weeks helped me understand the process of developing a research problem more clearly. I learned that a good research study needs a clear problem, measurable objectives, aligned research questions, appropriate variables, and a realistic scope. I also learned that revisions are a normal part of research. Although I found the process confusing at first, especially when checking whether our questions were quantitative and properly aligned, I became more confident after reviewing the concepts and applying them to our own research titles.

This experience taught me that research is not about making complicated questions. It is about asking the right questions about a real problem and making sure that those questions can actually be answered through data. I believe this lesson will help me as we continue developing our research proposal and eventually conduct the actual study.
